## I. INTRODUCTION

Many multi-robot missions are defined not only by the locations that robots must visit, but also by the temporal structure under which service events must occur. In a structured facility, for example, a group of transport robots may first move an item to an intermediate buffer, another robot type may then perform verification or handoff, and a downstream operation may start only after the prerequisite event has been completed. Similar service chains arise in logistics, inspection, and maintenance-support scenarios, where tasks involve precedence, waiting, synchronization, and repeated execution over multiple regions. These mission structures make linear temporal logic (LTL) a natural specification language, since LTL can describe temporal requirements over team behavior without assigning each subtask to a specific robot in advance [?].

This paper studies heterogeneous multi-robot task allocation under a global LTL specification, referred to as LTL-MRTA [?]. Each action proposition is associated with a type-level execution requirement, including the required robot type, the required number of robots, and the target region. The LTL formula specifies the global mission, while the planner decides which individual robots should execute each required action. This setting is flexible, but it couples temporal progress, robot assignment, and motion-induced cost. The resulting search space grows with the formula-derived automaton, the workspace size, the number of robots, and the synchronization requirements among collaborative subtasks.

In large instances, it is often important to reduce planning latency. A planner should be able to return an executable accepting plan early, while still preserving the ability to improve the plan quality when additional computation is available. This creates a tension between fast first-solution generation and systematic optimization. A purely greedy planner may quickly find a feasible plan but produce a poor makespan, whereas a globally optimizing method may spend significant time before returning any feasible solution. The goal of this work is to address both aspects within a unified planning framework.

Existing approaches to LTL-based multi-robot planning can be broadly classified into bottom-up and top-down methods. Bottom-up methods assign local temporal-logic specifications to individual robots and coordinate the team through communication, synchronization, or local replanning [?], [?]. These methods are effective when the global mission can be naturally distributed into robot-wise tasks. In contrast, top-down methods start from a global temporal-logic formula that specifies the team mission as a whole. This paper focuses on the top-down setting, where robot assignments are not fixed by the formula and must be decided jointly with temporal-task planning.

A classical top-down approach translates the global LTL formula into a nondeterministic Buchi automaton (NBA), constructs a product automaton with the robot transition systems, and then solves a graph-search or optimization problem on the product [?], [?]. This approach is principled, but the product space can become very large as the task automaton, workspace, and robot team grow. Sampling-based methods improve scalability by exploring the product space incrementally rather than constructing it explicitly [?], [?]. These methods provide important completeness and optimality guarantees under suitable assumptions, but they often rely on task-to-robot choices being specified or represented before search. If all admissible robot combinations are encoded directly into the LTL formula, the specification itself may become excessively long.

Optimization-based methods, including mixed-integer programming formulations, provide another expressive way to encode temporal and assignment constraints [?], [?]. They are valuable for bounded problems with rich constraints, but may become computationally demanding in large heterogeneous teams. Planning-tree and decision-tree methods reduce planning latency by expanding task progress and assignment decisions incrementally [?], [?]. They are particularly attractive when a feasible plan is needed quickly. However, because such methods often use greedy or locally guided decisions, the first feasible plan may not be the best one, and continued improvement remains important.

Branch-and-bound (BnB) is a natural tool for improving solution quality once an incumbent solution and a compact search structure are available. In temporal-logic multi-robot planning, BnB has been combined with automaton-derived task structures or partial-order abstractions to search over robot assignments and schedules [?], [?]. Its practical effectiveness depends on reaching a useful incumbent early: if a planner must first construct a large automaton or extract a complex planning abstraction before any feasible plan is found, then BnB cannot reduce the initial planning latency. This observation motivates methods that connect feasible-plan discovery with the construction of the planning structure itself.

Another line of work improves scalability by restructuring or decomposing the task specification. Decomposition-based methods extract subtasks and temporal relations to exploit parallelism [?], [?]. Hierarchical temporal-logic representations, such as H-LTLf, improve interpretability and planning efficiency by allowing complex missions to be specified through hierarchical task structures [?]. Poset-product methods avoid translating a long conjunction of task formulas into a single monolithic automaton by composing relaxed partial-order abstractions [?]. These approaches are powerful and complementary, especially when a mission has a useful hierarchy or can be decomposed into subformulas with manageable interactions.

However, long temporal specifications are not always amenable to efficient decomposition into independent components. Shared atomic propositions, nested temporal dependencies, disjunctive choices, until-style waiting constraints, synchronized collaborative subtasks, and prefix-suffix coupling can make the interactions among subformulas important for planning. In such cases, specification-level decomposition alone may not fully remove the burden of jointly reasoning about automaton progress and heterogeneous robot assignment. This paper therefore takes a different but complementary direction: we keep the standard global LTL formulation and make automaton generation itself planning-aware.

We propose a planning-aware automaton-generation framework for heterogeneous LTL-MRTA. The method follows the LTL2BA-style translation pipeline, where an LTL formula is transformed through intermediate automata before obtaining an NBA [?]. Instead of first constructing the complete automaton and then performing planning as a separate stage, the proposed method introduces robot-team, workspace, and partial-planning information during automaton construction. In the generalized Buchi automaton (GBA) stage, feasibility information is used to filter transitions that cannot be realized by the available heterogeneous team. In the NBA stage, automaton expansion is interleaved with the construction of a shared plan graph.

In this work, "on-the-fly" denotes the computation-stage procedure in which automaton states and planning-graph nodes are generated incrementally for a fixed global LTL mission. Execution-time task release, robot failure recovery, and dynamic replanning are outside the main scope of this paper.

The shared plan graph stores partial plans that combine automaton progress with task-level execution information, including action requirements, robot assignments, predicted robot positions, completion times, and local cost estimates. To reduce redundant suffix expansion, the graph uses a task-level semantic-signature criterion: plan nodes with compatible Buchi-state, phase, and transition-label signatures can share successor structure when they represent the same task-level continuation. This criterion compacts repeated suffix structure without asserting equivalence of full robot execution states, and late-arriving anchors can connect to already generated successor frontiers. After graph construction, dead-end branches that cannot reach accepting terminal nodes are removed through live-corridor simplification, leaving a compact planning space for subsequent optimization.

Once a first feasible accepting plan is found, its makespan becomes an incumbent for a warm-start BnB procedure. The first feasible plan warm-starts branch-and-bound, which then improves solution quality within the generated shared plan-graph assignment space and returns the best incumbent available under the given computational budget. In this way, the proposed framework combines fast first-solution generation with continued solution improvement, without relying on a prior hierarchical decomposition of the input formula.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

1. We develop a planning-aware automaton-generation framework for heterogeneous LTL-MRTA under a global LTL specification. Robot-team feasibility, workspace information, and intermediate planning information are incorporated during GBA/NBA construction, rather than only after a complete automaton has been generated.

2. We construct an NBA and a shared plan graph in an interleaved manner. The plan graph combines automaton progress with task-assignment and robot-state information, and uses feasibility filtering, task-level semantic-signature-based sharing, late-anchor successor inheritance, local cost-guided expansion, lower-bound evaluation, and live-corridor simplification to reduce redundant suffix expansion and expose a compact planning space for optimization.

3. We propose a warm-start branch-and-bound procedure over the generated planning space. The first feasible accepting plan provides an incumbent, and the subsequent BnB search improves the makespan within the shared plan graph.

Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method can obtain feasible accepting plans with reduced planning latency and further improve makespan through warm-start branch-and-bound refinement, especially for complex temporal specifications and large heterogeneous robot teams.
