\section{INTRODUCTION}
Multi-robot systems (MRS) are increasingly deployed in applications such as persistent surveillance \cite{leahy2016persistent}, logistics \cite{LiRAL23}, and autonomous maintenance \cite{liu2024time}. Missions in these domains are rarely unordered collections of independent goals; they may include precedence, recurrence, safety, and collaboration requirements. For example, in warehouse inspection, information about incoming goods may need to be retrieved before subsequent inspections are performed, while checking outgoing goods may require several robots to act together. Linear temporal logic (LTL) provides a compact formal language for specifying such requirements \cite{baier2008principles,schillinger2018decomposition,11627658}, giving rise to LTL-based multi-robot task allocation (LTL-MRTA) \cite{luo2022temporal}.

In the LTL-MRTA setting considered here, a team mission is specified by a global LTL formula over region and action propositions, while the identities of the robots assigned to the required actions are not fixed by the formula. The planner must determine these assignments while respecting the temporal and collaborative requirements and accounting for the robots' capabilities and motion costs. This problem becomes increasingly difficult as the robot team and the task specification grow, because both the automaton induced by the formula and the associated assignment space may expand rapidly. In practice, two performance objectives are therefore important: the end-to-end time required to obtain the first feasible plan and the quality improvement achieved with additional computation. Rather than waiting for an optimized solution before execution can be considered, it is desirable to expose a feasible incumbent as soon as one is found and to improve it thereafter.

A common top-down pipeline first translates the global LTL formula into an automaton or another task representation and then performs task allocation and optimization over the resulting structure \cite{ulusoy2013optimality,luo2022temporal,liu2024time}. This separation provides a modular interface between temporal-logic translation and planning, but it also makes representation construction part of the end-to-end latency to the first feasible plan. Recent planning-decision-tree, anytime-poset, and structured-specification methods have already made substantial progress toward early solution generation \cite{Chen2024RealTimeReactive,10530927,liu2024time,research0337}. The remaining question addressed in this work is therefore narrower: can information about team feasibility, predicted robot execution states, and partial assignments be introduced directly into a standard LTL-to-automaton pipeline, so that allocation can proceed while the task automaton is still being generated?

To investigate this question, we develop a planning-aware LTL-MRTA framework based on the LTL2BA translation pipeline \cite{Gastin2001FastLT}. During generalized B\"uchi automaton (GBA) construction, candidate transitions are screened against the robot types and cardinalities available in the team, and transitions that cannot be realized are discarded. Progress-consistent decomposable transitions are subsequently removed to avoid carrying unnecessary synchronization into the next stage. During the conversion from the GBA to a nondeterministic B\"uchi automaton (NBA), the NBA and a plan graph are constructed synchronously. Each newly generated NBA transition induces a subtask, whose assignment is evaluated from the robot positions and completion times stored in the current plan node. Feasible extensions are recorded in the plan graph, while a residual-obligation score orders pending successors using the current plan cost and an estimate of the remaining task burden.

Once the joint construction reaches a plan node that represents a feasible accepting prefix--suffix structure, the corresponding plan can be returned immediately without requiring prior completion of the remaining NBA construction. If such a node is encountered before the NBA is complete, the method therefore exposes an executable incumbent earlier than a complete-automaton-first implementation of the same planning pipeline. Construction then continues, and the plan-graph solution is updated whenever a lower-cost accepting plan is found. After the plan graph has been completed, its best solution warm-starts a branch-and-bound optimizer for further improvement. The retained plan graph can also be re-rooted at the last confirmed execution state when a robot becomes unavailable, allowing the unfinished assignments to be recomputed for the surviving team. The same graph thus supports both nominal optimization and task reallocation.

The proposed architecture does not eliminate the worst-case growth of LTL automata or robot-assignment search. Its purpose is instead to change when planning information enters the translation pipeline and when a feasible incumbent can become available. Accordingly, the experimental evaluation separates the effect of GBA-level pruning, joint NBA/plan-graph construction, score-guided expansion, subsequent optimization, and graph-based replanning.

\subsection{Related Work}
Existing multi-robot planning methods under LTL specifications can first be distinguished by the scope of their specifications. Bottom-up approaches assign local LTL specifications to individual robots and obtain team behavior through communication, synchronization, or online reconfiguration. Guo et al. \cite{guo2015multi}, for example, developed a decentralized framework with online plan reconfiguration, while Yu et al. \cite{2022Distributed} proposed distributed motion coordination with online conflict resolution. Top-down approaches instead begin with a global temporal-logic specification for the entire team. This paper considers the latter setting. Within this setting, the distinction most relevant to first-plan latency is what temporal or task representation must be available before allocation begins and whether robot-level planning information affects the construction of that representation.

Classical top-down methods translate the global LTL formula into an NBA, construct a product with the robots' transition systems, and search the resulting graph for an accepting execution \cite{smith2010optimal,ulusoy2013optimality}. Their main limitation is the growth of the product state space with the workspace, the robot team, and the specification automaton. Sampling-based methods alleviate this burden by incrementally exploring trees over the synchronous-product space instead of explicitly constructing the entire product \cite{kantaros2018sampling,kantaros2020stylus}. These methods substantially improve scalability and provide probabilistic completeness and asymptotic optimality under their respective assumptions. Their principal mechanism, however, is incremental exploration of the product space; it is different from using task-allocation feedback during the LTL-to-automaton translation itself.

Other approaches reduce the assignment and optimization burden after obtaining a temporal task representation. The simultaneous task allocation and planning framework decomposes finite-LTL missions into parallelizable subtasks and integrates them with a team model \cite{schillinger2017multi,schillinger2018simultaneous}. Optimization-based methods encode temporal, allocation, and collaboration constraints in mixed-integer programs \cite{karaman2008vehicle,luo2022temporal,9663414}; for example, Luo and Zavlanos \cite{luo2022temporal} separate task planning from motion planning to reduce dependence on the workspace size. These formulations provide expressive constraint handling and can optimize assignment quality, although their time to the first feasible solution depends on the resulting model and solver. Hierarchical temporal-logic methods further reduce planning complexity by exposing task structure explicitly in the specification \cite{luo2024decomposition,luo2025simultaneous}.

Closely related work avoids constructing a B\"uchi automaton for the complete mission by exploiting structured specifications. Liu et al. \cite{research0337} consider sc-LTL missions supplied as conjunctions of subformulas, convert the subformulas into relaxed posets, compute poset products on the fly, and assign the resulting subtasks as new tasks are released. This method directly shares the objective of reducing preprocessing before a usable plan is obtained. Its temporal representation and input structure differ from those considered here: it builds online products of posets for an explicitly conjunctive specification, whereas the present method embeds team-aware planning into the GBA-to-NBA stage of a standard LTL2BA translation without requiring the mission to be supplied as a hierarchy or an explicit collection of subformulas.

Planning-decision-tree methods incrementally represent task progression and robot assignments and can generate feasible plans efficiently for large heterogeneous teams \cite{Chen2024RealTimeReactive,10530927,li2025task,11441465}. Several of these methods obtain their initial allocations through greedy expansion. The present framework also uses greedy allocation during its joint construction stage; its distinction is that the resulting planning state is used to order NBA generation and that the retained plan graph subsequently supports branch-and-bound refinement. Liu et al. \cite{liu2024time} take another anytime approach: partial-order relations are extracted from a task automaton and passed to a branch-and-bound assignment search, so that feasible solutions can be improved as computation continues. Their framework also includes online synchronization and reassignment of unfinished tasks after robot failures. Thus, early feasible planning, continued improvement, and failure adaptation are not individually unique to the present work. The relevant difference is the stage at which allocation begins: the proposed framework evaluates assignments during GBA-to-NBA construction, whereas the poset-based pipeline begins assignment from temporal structures extracted from an already translated task automaton. For either pipeline, a fair first-plan comparison should include all representation construction and preprocessing that precede the first feasible assignment.

Learning-based methods provide a different way to reduce deployment-time planning latency. For example, LTL2Action \cite{vaezipoor2021ltl2action} conditions a learned policy on temporal-logic instructions and avoids explicit automaton construction during deployment. Such methods shift computation to policy training and address different tradeoffs in formal guarantees, generalization, and adaptation to changes in the robot team; they are therefore complementary to the graph-search and optimization methods considered here.

In summary, existing work already provides incremental product exploration, structured and on-the-fly task decomposition, fast greedy planning, anytime optimization, and failure adaptation. The specific position of the proposed method is to introduce team-feasibility checks and evolving robot execution states inside the LTL2BA construction pipeline---at GBA filtering and GBA-to-NBA expansion---and to construct a plan graph that links the first feasible incumbent with subsequent optimization. This stage of coupling, rather than early planning or repair alone, is the distinction examined in this paper.
