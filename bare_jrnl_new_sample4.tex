\begin{abstract}
 This paper studies multi-robot task allocation under global linear temporal logic (LTL) specifications for heterogeneous robot teams. In conventional methods, task allocation typically begins only after the automaton has been constructed, which can substantially delay the first feasible plan, particularly for complex tasks. To address these limitations,  we propose an on-the-fly task allocation framework that introduces task allocation into the LTL-to-automaton translation process. Planning-related information is introduced into automaton generation to prune infeasible and unnecessary transitions before they are propagated to subsequent stages. More importantly, the plan graph is constructed on the fly together with the automaton, such that each newly generated transition is immediately exploited for task allocation. A scoring strategy further guides plan-graph expansion toward promising branches, enabling a high-quality feasible plan to be obtained
before the complete automaton is constructed. The resulting plan-graph solution is subsequently refined through branch-and-bound optimization, while the retained plan graph also supports rapid replanning when robots
become unavailable. Under the stated assumptions, the proposed framework is shown to be sound and complete. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed method obtains feasible and high-quality plans substantially earlier than state-of-the-art methods while maintaining favorable scalability as task and team sizes increase. Physical experiments further validate the executability of the generated plans and rapid task reallocation under robot failure.

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Multi-robot systems (MRS) have been widely used in applications such as persistent surveillance\cite{leahy2016persistent}, logistics\cite{LiRAL23}, and autonomous maintenance\cite{liu2024time}, where tasks often involve complex temporal requirements. Consider, for example, a warehouse inspection task where heterogeneous robots carry out a sequence of operations including inspecting incoming goods, examining stored goods, and verifying outgoing goods before dispatch. Such tasks often involve both temporal dependencies and multi-robot coordination. For instance, goods information may need to be retrieved from the central control area before subsequent inspections are carried out, whereas verifying outgoing goods may require robots to work together. Linear temporal logic (LTL) provides a compact means of specifying these constraints in a rigorous way \cite{baier2008principles,schillinger2018decomposition,11627658}. This gives rise to the LTL-based multi-robot task allocation (LTL-MRTA) problem. In LTL-MRTA, the task is specified by an LTL formula, but the specific robots assigned to each subtask are not predetermined by the formula\cite{luo2022temporal}.

 The LTL-MRTA problem becomes increasingly difficult as the number of robots and the complexity of the task specification grow, since the associated search and optimization space may expand explosively. As a result, many existing approaches, such as  mixed-integer linear programming (MILP)-based methods\cite{luo2022temporal, 9663414} and  search-based methods\cite{ulusoy2013optimality, kantaros2020stylus}, can require substantial computation time in large-scale problems, even when only a first feasible solution is required. In practice, however, obtaining a first feasible solution quickly is often critical since task execution often cannot wait for lengthy planning, especially when robot failures or other execution changes require rapid online replanning. It is therefore desirable to obtain a feasible solution as early as possible while retaining the ability to improve its quality as additional computation becomes available.

These methods enable online replanning. However, their task-allocation decisions are mainly greedy and can therefore be prone to local optima. Thus, while a feasible plan can be obtained rapidly, its solution quality may be limited, making further improvement desirable when additional computation time is available. This naturally motivates an anytime planning strategy that provides an executable solution early and progressively improves it as computation continues. The poset-based method \cite{liu2024time} follows this idea by extracting partial-order relations among subtasks and applying a branch-and-bound search to improve the task assignment over time, while also supporting online adaptation during task execution.

Poset-based methods \cite{liu2024time}, in contrast, support continued solution improvement by exploiting partial-order relations among subtasks and applying branch-and-bound optimization over task assignments. Together, these studies highlight the importance of both rapid feasibility and subsequent solution improvement.

These observations motivate us to reconsider whether the conventional pipeline of constructing the automaton first and planning afterward can itself be reorganized.
Inspired by the ``on-the-fly'' idea in \cite{Gastin2001FastLT}, a natural question arises: can task allocation be introduced while the automaton is being generated, rather than waiting until its construction is complete?
 
To this end, we propose a novel framework that integrates robot planning into the automaton generation process. Rather than first constructing the automaton and then performing planning on top of it, our approach introduces information about the robot team, the task and the workspace during the translation from LTL to automaton. Specifically, following the LTL2BA\cite{Gastin2001FastLT} translation pipeline, the framework prunes redundant and infeasible transitions during generalized Büchi automaton (GBA) construction. 
During GBA-to-NBA construction, a plan graph is generated synchronously with the NBA. Each subtask induced by a newly generated NBA transition is  assigned to feasible robots and incorporated into the plan graph. To obtain a higher-quality initial solution, the plan graph is expanded according to a residual-obligation score that favors feasible successors with lower current costs and smaller estimated burdens for the remaining parts. Consequently, the first feasible plan becomes available as soon as an accepting prefix--suffix structure is generated, before the complete NBA has been constructed. After the plan graph has been completed, a branch-and-bound optimizer is used to further improve the solution.  When a robot becomes unavailable during execution, the method re-roots the retained plan graph at the last confirmed execution state and re-optimizes the unfinished assignments over the remaining team.

\subsection{Related Work}
% 先总起讲大概背景
Existing planning methods under LTL constraints can generally be classified into bottom-up and top-down approaches. Bottom-up approaches usually assign local LTL specifications to individual robots and achieve team-level behavior through coordination, synchronization, and online reconfiguration\cite{guo2015multi, 2022Distributed, 10545569}. In contrast, top-down approaches start from a global LTL formula that specifies the overall team mission. This paper focuses on the latter setting.

For top-down methods, a common strategy is to construct the product automaton between the NBA translated from the global LTL formula and the robots' discrete transition systems and then solve the resulting graph-search problem \cite{smith2010optimal,Kloetzer2015LTL,9196928,DING2014399}.  However, this approach suffers from state explosion because the number of states in the product automaton increases rapidly with the workspace size, the number of robots, and the complexity of the task. Sampling-based methods \cite{kantaros2020stylus,kantaros2018sampling,9385707} alleviate this burden by incrementally approximating the product space rather than constructing it explicitly. However, such methods typically require the global LTL formula to explicitly assign subtasks to robots. When the robot assignment is not given a priori, a possible workaround is to enumerate all admissible robot combinations, but this can result in exponentially long LTL formulas. 
To further improve computational efficiency, some works decompose global LTL specifications and integrate task decomposition with allocation and planning, as in the simultaneous task allocation and planning (STAP) framework \cite{7989094, schillinger2017multi,schillinger2018simultaneous}. Other works formulate temporal-logic task planning as mixed-integer linear programs \cite{luo2022temporal,karaman2008vehicle,9663414, Raman2015MILP}. In particular, to mitigate the impact of workspace size on the computational burden, \cite{luo2022temporal} proposed a hierarchical architecture that separates task planning from motion planning. Although these methods can substantially reduce computational cost and improve scalability, they may still require considerable computation time in large-scale problems, even when only a first feasible solution is required.

% To avoid the combinatorial burden and reduced flexibility caused by explicitly allocating tasks to robots, some works decompose the global LTL specifications. For example, \cite{schillinger2017multi,schillinger2018simultaneous} proposed the simultaneous task allocation and planning (STAP) framework, which constructs a team model that integrates task decomposition and task allocation. By decomposing the global task into parallelizable subtasks, the framework improves planning efficiency. However, these works do not explicitly address tightly coupled collaborative tasks requiring the simultaneous participation of multiple robots. Other works address global temporal-logic task planning by using mixed-integer linear programming (MILP)\cite{luo2022temporal,karaman2008vehicle,9663414}.  Unfortunately, MILP-based methods can become computationally demanding in large-scale settings, even when only a first feasible solution is required. 
In practice, obtaining a feasible solution quickly is sometimes more valuable than spending substantially more time searching for an optimal one. Several studies have recognized this practical need.
Planning-decision-tree-based methods\cite{Chen2024RealTimeReactive, 2025Task,10168225 } represent task progression and task allocation incrementally, enabling rapid identification of feasible solutions and real-time planning for large-scale multi-robot systems. Although such methods are highly efficient, their reliance on greedy task-allocation strategies can make the resulting solutions prone to local optima. Poset-based methods \cite{liu2024time}, in contrast, support continued solution improvement by exploiting partial-order relations among subtasks and applying branch-and-bound optimization over task assignments. These methods have substantially improved the efficiency of LTL-MRTA planning. However, they mainly target the planning and allocation stages after the atomaton has been constructed, while the preceding temporal-logic translation process can still contribute significantly to the time required to obtain the feasible plan.


Some work reduces the burden of complex specifications by changing or exploiting the structure of the task formula\cite{luo2024decomposition,2025Nl2Hltl2Plan, Finkbeiner2022}. Hierarchical temporal-logic specifications \cite{luo2025simultaneous} organize complex specifications into a hierarchy, while \cite{research0337} exploit the case where an sc-LTL task is given as a conjunction of subformulas. While effective, these methods often require additional effort to design or reformulate the task specification into a suitable form, and their efficiency depends not only on the decomposability of the specification but also on the particular decomposition adopted\cite{Specification}. Another line of work employs learning-based methods to avoid explicit automaton construction during deployment\cite{vaezipoor2021ltl2action, 9341325, 9803859, 10167731, 11570807}. These approaches encode LTL formulas or use LTL progression to learn task-conditioned representations and policies, allowing temporal-logic reasoning to be partially amortized over training and enabling rapid inference for previously unseen specifications. However, the performance generally depends on the distribution of training tasks and the learned proposition or environment representation.

Overall, existing studies have made substantial and effective efforts to reduce the computational burden of LTL-MRTA. A remaining source of feasible-solution latency, however, arises in approaches where temporal-logic processing and robot allocation are still performed sequentially. In such pipelines, robot allocation cannot begin until the required task or automaton structure has been generated, so the time spent on this preceding stage directly contributes to the delay in obtaining a feasible solution. Moreover, because robot-team feasibility and planning information are introduced only afterward, infeasible or unnecessary structures may still be generated and propagated before allocation begins.

\subsection{Contributions}

This work makes several key contributions. First, it introduces an on-the-fly LTL-MRTA framework. Unlike conventional methods that construct the complete automation before allocation, the framework performs task allocation during automaton generation. Newly generated NBA transitions are immediately used for task allocation, with a residual-obligation score guiding the search toward promising branches. This enabes a high-quality feasible plan to generate before the complete NBA is constructed. The resulting plan graph is retained for rapid replanning when robots become unavailable. The second contribution is the incorporation of planning-related information before NBA construction to prune infeasible and redundant transitions and the associated states. This pruning prevents unnecessary states and transitions from being propagated to the subsequent NBA construction and planning stages. The third contribution is a theoretical analysis establishing the soundness and completeness of the proposed algorithm under assumptions on robot motion.

\section{PRELIMINARIES}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
\subsection{Linear Temporal Logic}

Linear temporal logic (LTL) \cite{baier2008principles} is widely used to specify temporal tasks over a set of atomic propositions $\mathcal{AP}$. Following the standard syntax, an LTL formula over $\mathcal{AP}$ is recursively defined by
\[
\varphi := \top \mid \pi \mid \neg \varphi \mid \varphi_1 \wedge \varphi_2 \mid \bigcirc \varphi \mid \varphi_1 \,\mathcal{U}\, \varphi_2,
\]
where $\pi \in \mathcal{AP}$, $\top$ denotes the constant true, $\neg$ and $\wedge$ are the negation and conjunction operators, respectively, $\bigcirc$ is the next operator, and $\mathcal{U}$ is the until operator. Other temporal operators can be derived in the standard way, including eventually $\lozenge \varphi := \top \,\mathcal{U}\, \varphi$ and always $\square \varphi := \neg \lozenge \neg \varphi$.

Let $\Sigma = 2^{\mathcal{AP}}$ be the alphabet. An infinite word over $\Sigma$ is a sequence $w=\sigma_0\sigma_1\cdots \in \Sigma^\omega$, where $\omega$ denotes an infinite repetition and  $\sigma_k \in \Sigma$. The notation $w \models \varphi$ means that the word $w$ satisfies the LTL formula $\varphi$.  The language of $\varphi$ is defined as $\mathrm{Words}(\varphi) := \{\, w \in \Sigma^\omega \mid w \models \varphi \,\}.$
An LTL formula can be translated into an NBA.
\begin{definition}[Nondeterministic Büchi Automaton]
A nondeterministic Büchi automaton (NBA) is a tuple
$$
\mathcal{B}_{\varphi}=(Q_B,Q_{B,0},\Sigma,\rightarrow_B,Q_{B,F}),
$$
where $Q_B$ is a finite set of states, $Q_{B,0} \subseteq Q_B$ is the set of initial states, $\rightarrow_B \subseteq Q_B \times \Sigma \times Q_B$ is the transition relation, and $Q_{B,F} \subseteq Q_B$ is the set of accepting states.
\end{definition}
An infinite run of $\mathcal{B}_\varphi$ over a word $w=\sigma_0\sigma_1\cdots$ is a sequence $\rho=q_0q_1\cdots$ such that $q_0 \in Q_{B,0}$ and $(q_k,\sigma_k,q_{k+1}) \in \rightarrow_B$ for all $k \ge 0$, where each $\sigma_k \in \Sigma$. The run is accepting if it visits accepting states infinitely often, i.e., $\mathrm{Inf}(\rho)\cap Q_{B,F} \neq \emptyset$. For a satisfiable LTL formula, an accepting run can be written in the prefix--suffix form, where the prefix reaches an accepting state once and the suffix repeats a cycle around that accepting state indefinitely.

\subsection{LTL2BA Translation}

In this work, we adopt the LTL2BA translation pipeline \cite{Gastin2001FastLT}, a classical and widely used procedure for translating LTL specifications into nondeterministic Büchi automata. Given an LTL formula $\varphi$, LTL2BA first converts it into negative normal form and applies rewriting rules to simplify the formula. An abstract syntax tree is then constructed from the simplified formula for subsequent automaton generation.

% \begin{definition}[Abstract Syntax Tree]
% Given an LTL formula $\varphi$ in negative normal form, its abstract syntax tree (AST), denoted by $\mathcal{D}_\varphi$, is a rooted ordered tree in which each internal node is labeled by a logical or temporal operator, and each leaf is labeled by an atomic proposition or its negation. The recursive structure of $\mathcal{D}_\varphi$ represents the syntactic decomposition of $\varphi$ and provides the basis for extracting temporal subformulas during automaton construction.
% \end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Abstract Syntax Tree\cite{Gastin2001FastLT,2006Compilers}] Given an LTL formula $\varphi$ in negative normal form, its abstract syntax tree (AST), denoted by $\mathcal{D}_\varphi$, is a rooted ordered tree in which each internal node is labeled by a Boolean or temporal operator, and each leaf is labeled by a literal or a Boolean constant. The children of each internal node correspond, in order, to the operands of its operator. \end{definition}

Starting from the AST, LTL2BA first constructs a very weak alternating automaton (VWAA). 
% An alternating automaton may require multiple successor states to hold simultaneously under the same input symbol. Moreover, the ``very weak'' property imposes a partial order on states, so that transitions can only remain at the same level or move downward in this order, which yields a simpler automaton structure.

\begin{definition}[Very Weak Alternating Automaton]
A very weak alternating automaton (VWAA) is a tuple
\[
\mathcal{V}_\varphi=(Q_v,\Sigma,\delta_v,I_v,F_v,\preceq_v),
\]
where $Q_v$ is a finite set of states, $\delta_v$ is the transition function, $I_v$ is the initial condition, $F_v \subseteq Q_v$ is the set of accepting states, and $\preceq_v$ is a partial order on $Q_v$ such that every state appearing in the transition of a state $q_v \in Q_v$ is lower than or equal to $q_v$ under $\preceq_v$.
\end{definition}

Based on the VWAA, LTL2BA then constructs a generalized Büchi automaton (GBA), which preserves multiple acceptance requirements in a compact intermediate form before the final degeneralization step.

\begin{definition}[Generalized Büchi Automaton]
A generalized Büchi automaton (GBA) is a tuple
\[
\mathcal{G}_\varphi=(Q_g,Q_{g,0},\Sigma,\rightarrow_g,\mathcal{F}_g),
\]
where $Q_g$ is a finite set of states, $Q_{g,0} \subseteq Q_g$ is the set of initial states, $\Sigma$ is the input alphabet, $\rightarrow_g \subseteq Q_g \times \Sigma \times Q_g$ is the transition relation, and
$\mathcal{F}_g=\{F_{g,1},\dots,F_{g,m_g}\}$
is a finite collection of accepting transition sets with $F_{g,i} \subseteq \rightarrow_g$ for all $i \in \{1,\dots,m_g\}$. A run is accepting if, for every $F_{g,i} \in \mathcal{F}_g$, it traverses transitions in $F_{g,i}$ infinitely often.
\end{definition}

Following \cite{Gastin2001FastLT}, each GBA state \(q_g \in Q_g \) represents a conjunction of VWAA states. Outgoing GBA transitions are generated by combining one outgoing transition from each constituent VWAA state: their conditions are conjoined and their successor sets are merged to form the successor GBA state. Each consistent combination yields a candidate transition, which is simplified using the implication-based rules of LTL2BA before being added to the GBA. The resulting GBA is then degeneralized into an NBA by augmenting each GBA state with an acceptance-progress index that tracks the accepting sets visited during execution. The overall translation pipeline is $ \varphi \rightarrow \mathcal{D}_\varphi \rightarrow \mathcal{V}_\varphi
\rightarrow \mathcal{G}_\varphi \rightarrow \mathcal{B}_\varphi. $

\section{PROBLEM DESCRIPTION}
\label{sec:problem_description}

\subsection{Workspace and Heterogeneous Robot Team}

Consider a  bounded workspace $\mathcal{W}$ containing $n_l$ regions of interest $\mathcal{L}=\{l_1,l_2,\ldots,l_{n_l}\}$. Let $\mathcal{O}= \{o_1,o_2,\ldots,o_{n_o}\}$ denote the set of $n_o$ obstacle and forbidden regions.
The regions of interest are mutually disjoint and do not overlap with any obstacle or forbidden region, i.e., $l_i\cap o_j=\emptyset,\quad \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n_l\},\ \forall j\in\{1,\ldots,n_o\}$, $l_i\cap l_j=\emptyset,\quad \forall i\neq j $. 

A team of $n_r$ heterogeneous robots is denoted by $ \mathcal{R}=\{r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_{n_r}\}.$
The robots belong to a finite set of types $\mathcal{T}=\{1,2,\ldots,n_t\},$ where each type represents a specific capability, such as sensing or manipulation. Each robot belongs to exactly one type. Let $\tau(r_k)\in\mathcal{T}$ denote the type of robot $r_k$, and let $\mathcal{R}_j=\{r_k\in\mathcal{R}\mid \tau(r_k)=j\}$ be the set of robots of type $j$. The position of robot $r_k$ is denoted by $x(r_k)\in\mathcal W$, and its constant travel speed is denoted by $v(r_k)>0$. For each robot $r_k \in \mathcal{R}$, its motion between regions is modeled by a transition graph $\mathcal G_{k}=(V_{r,k},\xrightarrow{}_{r,k})$, where $V_{r,k}\subseteq\mathcal L$ is the set of regions accessible to robot $r_k$ and $\xrightarrow{}_{r,k} \subseteq V_{r,k}\times V_{r,k}$ denotes the set of region-to-region transitions permitted for robot $r_k$. The transition relation is assumed to be symmetric, and $\mathcal G_k$ is connected. Robots of the same type share the same transition graph.
For any position $x\in\mathcal W$ and target region $l_i\in\mathcal L$, let $d_k(x,l_i)$ denote the shortest feasible distance that robot $r_k$ must travel from $x$ to reach $l_i$. In particular, $d_k(x(r_k),l_i)$ is the distance from the current position of $r_k$ to $l_i$. We set $d_k(x,l_i)=+\infty$ if $l_i$ is unreachable by $r_k$.


\subsection{Task Specification}

The temporal-logic specification is defined over the atomic proposition set \(\mathcal{AP}\). 
Before formulating the temporal logic task, we introduce two classes of atomic propositions.  
(i) $ \mathcal{P} \triangleq \{p_i \mid l_i\in\mathcal{L}\}$  contains region propositions, where \(p_i\) is true when at least one available robot is located at
region $l_i$.
(ii) Let $a_i$ be an action proposition. For example, $a_i$ can represent an action in which a group of robots visits a specified target region.  Let
$ \mathcal{A}\triangleq \{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{n_{\rm act}}\} $
denote the set of $n_{\rm act}$ action propositions. Each $a_i\in\mathcal{A}$ is associated with an action requirement $ c(a_i)=(\tau(a_i),n(a_i),l(a_i)),  $
where $\tau(a_i)\in\mathcal{T}$ is the required robot type, $n(a_i)\in\mathbb{N}$ is the required number of robots of type $\tau(a_i)$, and $l(a_i)\in\mathcal{L}$ is the target region. The proposition $a_i$ is satisfied if and only if the corresponding action is performed by $n(a_i)$ robots of type $\tau(a_i)$ at region $l(a_i)$.
In the factory scenario, an action proposition can represent an operation such as inspection, material delivery, collaborative repair, or monitoring. The tuple $c(a_i)$ specifies the required robot capability, the number of robots that must participate, and the region where the operation must be completed.
The complete set of atomic propositions used in the temporal logic specification is defined as
$\mathcal{AP}\triangleq \mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{A}$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:warehouse}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{workspace}, a warehouse with eight regions of interest, denoted by $\mathcal{L}=\{l_1,l_2,\ldots,l_8\}$. $l_1$ and $l_4$ are inbound and outbound areas, respectively, while the remaining regions are used for goods storage. The robot team consists of five robots, $\mathcal{R}=\{r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4,r_5\}$, where $r_1,r_2,r_3$ are type-1 robots and $r_4,r_5$ are type-2 robots. Consider the inspection task $\varphi=\Diamond\left(a_1\wedge(\Diamond a_2\wedge\Diamond a_3)\right)\wedge\Box\neg p_8$.
Here, $c(a_1)=(2,2,l_5)$, $c(a_2)=(2,1,l_6)$, and $c(a_3)=(1,1,l_1)$.

After that, one type-2 robot must check the equipment-maintenance area $l_6$, and one type-1 robot must inspect the inbound receiving area $l_1$. 
The task first requires two type-2 robots to collaboratively inspect region $l_5$. After this inspection, one type-2 robot should perform an equipment check at $l_6$ and one type-1 robot should inspect the inbound area $l_1$. Throughout the task, all robots must avoid the restricted area $l_8$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Problem Definition} \label{pro_def}
Consider an LTL formula \(\varphi\) over \(\mathcal{AP}\).
% Let $\mathcal B_\varphi=(Q_B,Q_{B,0},\Sigma,\rightarrow_B,Q_{B,F})$ be the NBA associated with \(\varphi\). Here, \(Q_B\) is the finite set of B\"uchi states, \(Q_{B,0}\) is the set of initial states, \(\Sigma=2^{\mathcal{AP}}\) is the alphabet, \(\rightarrow_B\subseteq Q_B\times\Sigma\times Q_B\) is the transition relation, and \(Q_{B,F}\) is the set of accepting states.
 To connect the generated NBA $\mathcal B_\varphi$ with task-level planning, we associate each NBA transition with a subtask. 

\begin{definition}[Subtask]
Consider an NBA transition $(q_{B,i},\sigma_{ij},q_{B,j})\in\rightarrow_B$, where  \(q_{B,i},q_{B,j}\in Q_B\) are NBA states. Let $\sigma_i^{\rm s}$ denote the self-loop condition at NBA state $q_{B,i}$.   If no holding condition is required, we set \(\sigma_i^{\rm s}=\top\). The subtask induced by this  transition is defined as follows.
$$
\xi_{ij}=(\sigma_{ij},\sigma_i^{\rm s}).
$$
Here, $\sigma_{ij}$ specifies the propositions that enable the transition from $q_{B,i}$ to $q_{B,j}$, while
$\sigma_i^{\rm s}$ specifies the propositions that must be maintained before this transition is completed. For subsequent processing, each condition is represented as the positive and negated atomic propositions.
\end{definition}
Since the holding and negative region constraints associated with a subtask may restrict the robots' admissible motion, we make the following connectivity assumption.
\begin{assumption}[Robot motion connectivity] \label{assum:motion}
For every subtask $\xi_{ij}$, after excluding the regions prohibited by the holding condition $\sigma_i^s$ and the negative propositions in $\sigma_{ij}$, the transition graph of each robot over the remaining admissible regions remains connected. For example, in Example~1, although $\neg p_8$ prohibits the robots from entering or traversing region $l_8$, all remaining admissible regions are still mutually reachable.For instance, a robot can still travel between $l_1$ and $l_2$ without traversing $l_8$.
\end{assumption}


% For a subtask \(\xi_{ij}\), let \(\mathcal A(\xi_{ij})\subseteq\mathcal A\) be the set of action propositions that appear as positive requirements in the transition label \(\sigma_{ij}\).
A plan for $\varphi$ is defined as $\Pi=(\boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\rho}, \boldsymbol{\mathcal C})$, where $ \boldsymbol{\xi}=\xi_0\xi_1\xi_2\cdots$ is the corresponding subtask sequence, $\boldsymbol{\rho}=q_0q_1q_2\cdots$ is the corresponding NBA-state sequence, and $ \boldsymbol{\mathcal C}=\mathcal{C}_0\mathcal{C}_1\mathcal{C}_2\cdots$ is the corresponding robot-assignment sequence. Specifically,  for subtask $\xi_k$,  let $\mathcal A(\xi_k)\subseteq\mathcal A$ denote the set of action propositions that must be true required by its corresponding transition. The assignment $ \mathcal{C}_k:\mathcal A(\xi_k)\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal R} $ maps each required action proposition to the set of robots selected to execute it. Assignments for different action propositions in the same $\xi_k$ must be disjoint, i.e., $ \mathcal{C}_k(a_i)\cap \mathcal{C}_k(a_j)=\emptyset, \forall a_i\neq a_j,\ a_i,a_j\in\mathcal A(\xi_k). $

An accepting run of the NBA can be written in a prefix--suffix structure. Thus,  $\boldsymbol{\rho}$ can be 
written as $\boldsymbol{\rho}=\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathrm{pre}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathrm{suf}}\bigr)^\omega$ , where $\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the  prefix run and $\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathrm{suf}}$ is the suffix run. The corresponding plan $\Pi$ admits the same prefix--suffix structure, i.e. $\Pi = \Pi_{\mathrm{pre}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{suf}})^\omega$. Here, $ \Pi_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the plan segment associated with $\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathrm{pre}}$ , and $\Pi_{\mathrm{suf}}$ is the finite plan segment associated with one traversal of the suffix cycle $\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\mathrm{suf}}$. To represent one completed execution of the prefix--suffix plan, we define the finite plan  \(\Pi_{\mathrm{fin}} =
\Pi_{\mathrm{pre}}\Pi_{\mathrm{suf}}\). Let \(T(\Pi_{\mathrm{fin}})\) denote its makespan, i.e., the time required to complete \(\Pi_{\mathrm{fin}}\). 
For a prefix--suffix plan \(\Pi\), the cost is defined as follows.
\begin{equation} \label{objective}
J_\varphi=T(\Pi)=T(\Pi_{\mathrm{fin}}).
\end{equation}
% Thus, the plan cost $J_\varphi$ is the makespan, i.e., the completion time of $\Pi_{\mathrm{fin}}$.

\begin{problem}
Consider a  workspace $\mathcal{W}$ and a heterogeneous robot team $\mathcal{R}$ with type set $\mathcal{T}$, and an LTL formula $\varphi$ defined over the atomic proposition set $\mathcal{AP}=\mathcal{P} \cup \mathcal{A}$.  The goal is to find a plan $\Pi$ and the associated robot assignments such that $\varphi$ is satisfied and the cost $J_\varphi$ in Eq.~\eqref{objective} is minimized.
\end{problem}

\section{Pruning for GBA} \label{sec:gba_pruning}

By removing infeasible and redundant transitions before NBA generation, the procedure reduces the GBA size and prevents unnecessary structures from being propagated to the subsequent NBA construction and planning stages. The pruning consists of the following two parts.

\subsubsection{Pruning of Progress-Consistent Decomposable Transitions}
After GBA construction, a second pruning step removes unnecessary synchronization among independent subtasks.
We first introduce a general definition of transition decomposability. The definition applies to both NBA and GBA transitions.


% The main challenge is to identify and remove such synchronization before the
% complete NBA is available. A GBA transition can therefore be removed only when decomposing its combined task requirements preserves the
% acceptance progress used in the subsequent GBA-to-NBA degeneralization. To formalize this decomposition without restricting it to a particular
% automaton type, we first introduce a general definition of transition decomposability. The definition applies to both NBA and GBA transitions.

\begin{definition}[Decomposable transition]
\label{def:decomposable_transition}
Consider an automaton with state set $Q$, alphabet $\Sigma$, and transition relation $\rightarrow\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q$.
A transition $\rightarrow_{ij}=(q_i,\sigma_{ij},q_j)\in\rightarrow$ is called decomposable if there exist a state $q_k\in Q$ and two
transitions $(q_i,\sigma_{ik},q_k)\in\rightarrow$ and $(q_k,\sigma_{kj},q_j)\in\rightarrow$ such that
$\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ik}\cup\sigma_{kj}$.
\end{definition}


% A decomposable NBA transition can be pruned because requiring several independent subtasks to be satisfied simultaneously by a single transition can be unnecessarily restrictive \cite{liu2024time}. For GBA pruning, however, decomposability alone is not sufficient because degeneralization also tracks progress through multiple accepting transition sets. We therefore call a decomposable GBA transition progress-consistent if its decomposition produces the same acceptance-progress update as the original transition for every degeneralization index. Only progress-consistent decomposable transitions are eligible for pruning.
A decomposable NBA transition can be pruned because requiring several independent subtasks to be satisfied simultaneously by a single transition can be unnecessarily restrictive \cite{liu2024time}. At the GBA level, however, decomposability alone is insufficient for pruning, since GBA-to-NBA degeneralization also tracks progress through multiple accepting transition sets. Applying the NBA pruning criterion directly to the GBA could therefore remove transitions whose decompositions alter the acceptance-progress update and thus do not preserve the original acceptance behavior. To avoid such incorrect pruning, we call a decomposable GBA transition progress-consistent if its decomposition produces the same acceptance-progress update as the original transition for every degeneralization index.
% Let $m_g:=|\mathcal{F}_g|$ and
% $\mathcal{F}_g=\{F_{g,1},\ldots,F_{g,m_g}\}$.
The accepting transition sets of the GBA is  $\mathcal{F}_g=\{F_{g,1},\ldots,F_{g,m_g}\}$. For an acceptance-progress index \(\ell\in\{0,\ldots,m_g\}\),  let
\(\eta(\ell,\rightarrow^g_{ij})\) denote the index reached after traversing \(\rightarrow^g_{ij}\). For \(\ell<m_g\),  define $ \eta(\ell,\rightarrow^g_{ij}) =\max\left\{ r\in\{\ell,\ldots,m_g\}\;\middle|\;\rightarrow^g_{ij}\in F_{g,h},  \forall h\in\{\ell+1,\ldots,r\}\right\}$. For \(\ell=m_g\), set $\eta(m_g,\rightarrow^g_{ij})=\eta(0,\rightarrow^g_{ij}).$ Here, $r$ denotes a candidate acceptance-progress index. The function
$\eta(\ell,\rightarrow^g_{ij})$ returns the largest index that can be reached after traversing \(\rightarrow^g_{ij}\). A decomposition is progress-consistent if its sequential progress update is identical to that of the original transition for every admissible initial progress index. Therefore, only progress-consistent decomposable
GBA transitions are eligible for pruning. The following definition formalizes the condition.



%  For  accepting transition sets  $\mathcal{F}_g=\{F_{g,1},\ldots,F_{g,m_g}\}$ and GBA transition $\rightarrow^g_{ij}\in\rightarrow_g$, define its acceptance-set index set as
% $\operatorname{Acc}(\rightarrow^g_{ij}):=

$(q_{g,k},\eta(\ell,\rightarrow^g_{ik}))$ and is therefore
decomposable.

\end{lemma}

$\Pi=(\boldsymbol{\xi},\rho,\mathbf{C})$, where $\rho$ is an accepting run.  A GBA transition removed by infeasible-transition pruning cannot induce

Lemma~\ref{l1}, each NBA transition induced by $\rightarrow^g_{ij}$ can be replaced by the two successive transitions induced by a progress-consistent decomposition
$(\rightarrow^g_{ik},\rightarrow^g_{kj})$. Since the GBA contains finitely many transitions, the recursive replacement terminates after finitely many steps. Therefore, $\mathcal{B}_{\varphi}^{-}$ also admits a feasible plan.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Plan Node}
The plan graph incrementally records feasible task-allocation results and their predicted execution states. Each plan node represents a partial plan and stores the corresponding task assignment and predicted robot execution state. The plan node is formally defined as follows.

% During NBA construction, feasible task allocations are
% incrementally recorded in a plan graph. Each plan node associates an NBA state with the corresponding robot assignment and predicted execution state. A plan node is formally defined as follows.
\begin{definition}[Plan node]\label{def:plan_node}
Consider the plan graph $G_P=(V_P,\rightarrow_P)$, where $V_P$ is the set of plan nodes and $\rightarrow_P\subseteq V_P\times V_P$ is the directed transition relation.
Each plan node is denoted by $\nu_i$, with $V_P=\{\nu_i\mid i=0,1,\ldots,N_P\}$, where $\nu_0$ is the root node. A plan node $\nu_i\in V_P$ is defined as
\[
\nu_i =
(q_B,\mathrm{Prog},\xi,\mathcal{C},X,\Theta).
\]
Here, \(q_B\in Q_B\) is the NBA state associated with \(\nu_i\), and \(\mathrm{Prog}\in\{\mathrm{pre},\mathrm{suf},\mathrm{acc},\mathrm{dead}\}\) denotes the planning status of \(\nu_i\). Specifically, \(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{pre}\) indicates that the current node is in the prefix stage and  \(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{suf}\) indicates that the current node is in the suffix stage.  \(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}\) indicates that the node completes an accepting prefix--suffix structure, while \(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{dead}\) indicates that the node represents a discarded branch and will not be expanded further. 
\(\xi\) is the subtask associated with \(\nu_i\), and \(\mathcal{C}\) is the corresponding robot assignment.
The vector $X=(x_i(r_1),x_i(r_2),\ldots,x_i(r_{n_r}))$ denotes the predicted robot positions, where \(x_i(r_k)\) is the predicted position of robot \(r_k\) at plan node \(\nu_i\). The vector
$\Theta=(\theta_i(r_1),\theta_i(r_2),\ldots,\theta_i(r_{n_r}))$
denotes the predicted completion times, where \(\theta_i(r_k)\) is the predicted completion time of robot \(r_k\).
\end{definition}

Overall, a plan node compactly represents the task progress, robot assignment, and predicted execution state of a partial plan, which are used for subsequent plan-graph construction.

The plan graph is constructed synchronously with the NBA. 
The root plan node $\nu_0$ is initialized by
$\nu_0.q_B=q_{B,0}\in Q_{B,0}$,
$\nu_0.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{pre}$,
$\nu_0.\xi=\emptyset$,

$\nu_0.X$ records the initial robot positions, and
$\nu_0.\Theta=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_r}$.
For a plan node $\nu_i$, its current cost is defined as
\begin{equation}
J(\nu_i)=\max_{r_k\in\mathcal R}\theta_i(r_k).
\label{eq:plan-node-cost}
\end{equation}
In particular, when $\nu_i.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:plan-node-cost} gives the cost of the corresponding accepting plan, i.e., $J(\nu_i)=J_\varphi$ in
Eq.~\eqref{objective}.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:otf_pg} summarizes the  construction procedure. During construction, $Q_B^{\rm open}$ stores the NBA states waiting to be expanded, whereas $Q_B^{\rm exp}$ contains the states that have already been expanded. When an NBA state $q_{B,i}$ is selected from $Q_B^{\rm open}$, its outgoing transitions are generated by degeneralizing the corresponding GBA transitions and simplified by removing transitions subsumed by other transitions generated from the same state~\cite{Gastin2001FastLT}.
For each generated transition
$(q_{B,i},\sigma_{ij},q_{B,j})\in\rightarrow_B$,
all plan nodes $\nu\in V_P$ satisfying $\nu.q_B=q_{B,i}$ are grouped by $\nu.\mathrm{Prog}\in\{\mathrm{pre},\mathrm{suf}\}$ and expanded independently according to the induced subtask $\xi_{ij}=(\sigma_{ij},\sigma_i^{\rm s})$ through \textsc{Extend\_PlanGraph}. For the same NBA transition, these plan nodes do not repeatedly create identical child nodes; instead, they are connected to a common child plan node $\nu'$ whenever the resulting planning status is the same.  For each newly generated NBA state $q_{B,j}$, an ordering score
$s_B(q_{B,j})$ is computed according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}. The newly generated NBA states are then inserted into $Q_B^{\rm open}$ in nondecreasing order of their scores, so that more promising branches are expanded earlier.  If $q_{B,j}\in Q_B^{\rm exp}$,  \textsc{Reuse\_Successors} connects $\nu'$ to the existing plan nodes previously generated from $q_{B,j}$, without reconstructing the corresponding part of the plan graph.
If the generated child node satisfies $\nu'.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$, the corresponding plan is extracted as
a candidate solution and updates $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ if $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ is empty or $J(\nu')<T(\Pi_{\rm plan})$.  After $Q_B^{\rm open}$ becomes empty, the resulting plan graph is simplified according to Section~\ref{subsec:final_pruning}. The algorithm then returns
the simplified plan graph $G_P^{-}$ together with the best accepting plan $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ found during construction.

\subsubsection{\textsc{Extend\_PlanGraph}} \label{gen}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:extend_successor} presents the function
\textsc{Extend\_PlanGraph}, which extends the plan graph based on the subtask induced by the NBA transition
$\rightarrow^B_{ij}=(q_{B,i},\sigma_{ij},q_{B,j})$ and self-loop $\rightarrow^B_{ii}=(q_{B,i},\sigma_{ii},q_{B,i})$.

Consider a parent plan node $\nu_i$ associated with $q_{B,i}$. The robot assignment of the child node $\nu'$ is calculated by a greedy strategy. For each required action proposition $a_\ell\in\mathcal A(\xi_{ij})$, the action requirement
$c(a_\ell)=(\tau(a_\ell),n(a_\ell),l(a_\ell))$ specifies the required robot type, number of robots, and target region. For every robot $r_k\in\mathcal R_{\tau(a_\ell)}$, its predicted arrival time $t(r_k,a_\ell)$ at $l(a_\ell)$ is calulated as
\begin{equation}
t(r_k,a_\ell) = \theta_{\nu_i}(r_k) + \frac{d_k(x_i(r_k),l(a_\ell))}{v(r_k)}.
\label{eq:predicted_arrival}
\end{equation} 
A robot is eligible for $a_\ell$ if its predicted arrival time is finite and it has not been assigned to another action proposition in the same subtask. The distance $d_k(x_{i}(r_k),l(a_\ell))$ is computed over the
feasible motion graph while respecting the holding constraints in $\sigma_{ii}$ and the negative propositions in $\sigma_{ij}$. For example, in Example~\ref{ex:warehouse},  $\neg p_8$ prevents the robots from traversing region $l_8$; hence, $l_8$ is treated as an obstacle when computing $d_k(x_{i}(r_k),l(a_\ell))$.
For each $a_\ell$, let $\mathcal R_{\mathrm{eli}}(a_\ell)$ denote the set of eligible robots for $a_\ell$. Let $t_i(a_\ell)$ denote the $n(a_\ell)$-th smallest value of $t_i(r_k,a_\ell)$ over $r_k\in\mathcal R_i^{\mathrm{eli}}(a_\ell)$. The predicted completion cost of the subtask $\xi_{ij}$ is calculated as
\begin{equation}
t_i(\xi_{ij})
=
\max_{a_\ell\in\mathcal A(\xi_{ij})}
t_i(a_\ell).
\label{eq:subtask_completion_time}
\end{equation}

For the NBA state $q_{B,i}$ and each $p\in\{\mathrm{pre},\mathrm{suf}\}$, let $\mathcal V_i^B(p)=\{\nu_i\in V_P\mid
\nu_i.q_B=q_{B,i},\,\nu_i.\mathrm{Prog}=p\}$. Each nonempty set $\mathcal V_i^B(p)$ is processed independently.  For each parent plan node $\nu_i\in\mathcal V_i^B(p)$, the greedy allocation described above is performed to compute the  cost $t_i(\xi_{ij})$. The  parent node with the smallest $t_i(\xi_{ij})$ is selected as the representative predecessor. If multiple parents have the same minimum cost, the first one in the evaluation order is selected. All feasible parents in the same set $\mathcal V_i^B(p)$ are connected to the common child node $\nu'$.
The child node $\nu'$ is then updated based on the robot assignment and predicted execution state obtained from $\nu^\star$, with $\operatorname{par}(\nu')=\nu^\star$ recorded for plan extraction.  Its assignment $\nu'.\mathcal C$ records the robots selected by the above greedy allocation. For each selected robot, its predicted completion time in $\nu'.\Theta$ is updated to $t_i(\xi_{ij})$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:subtask_completion_time}.
Robots not selected for $\xi_{ij}$ retain their predicted completion times and positions from $\nu^\star$. 
The planning status $\nu'.\mathrm{Prog}$ is updated according to the status of its parent node $\nu$ and the successor NBA state $q_{B,j}$. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\nu'.\mathrm{Prog}
=
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{pre},
& q_{B,j}\notin Q_{B,F},\ \nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{pre},\\
\mathrm{suf},
& q_{B,j}\in Q_{B,F},\ \nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{pre},\\
\mathrm{suf},
& q_{B,j}\notin Q_{B,F},\nu. \mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{suf},\\
\mathrm{acc},
& q_{B,j}\in Q_{B,F},\ \nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{suf}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The status $\mathrm{dead}$ denotes a discarded branch that is excluded from further expansion; hence, it does not participate in the above planning-status update.

\caption{\textsc{Extend\_PlanGraph}}
\label{alg:extend_successor}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE parent-node set $\mathcal V_i^B(p)$, successor NBA state $q_{B,j}$, subtask $\xi_{ij}$, robot team $\mathcal R$, and workspace $\mathcal W$.
\ENSURE child node $\nu^{'}$.
\IF{$\mathcal V_i^B(p)=\emptyset$}
    \RETURN $\emptyset$
\ENDIF
\FORALL{$\nu_i\in\mathcal V_i^B(p)$}

        \IF{$|\mathcal R_i^{\rm eli}(a_\ell)|<n(a_\ell)$}
            \STATE $\nu^{'}.\mathrm{Prog}\gets\mathrm{dead}$
            \STATE \text{break}
        \ENDIF

        \STATE Select the $n(a_\ell)$ eligible robots with the smallest predicted arrival times to obtain $t_i(a_\ell)$.
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Compute $t_i(\xi_{ij})$ according to
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:subtask_completion_time}
\ENDFOR

    \STATE Update $\nu'.\mathcal C$, $\nu'.X$, and $\nu'.\Theta$ according to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:predicted_arrival}--\eqref{eq:subtask_completion_time}.

    \ENDFOR

and insert $\nu'$ into $V_P$
    \RETURN $\nu'$
\end{algorithmic}

\subsubsection{\textsc{Reuse\_Successors}}

When a newly generated plan node $\nu_j$ is associated with an NBA state $q_{B,j}\in Q_B^{\rm exp}$, the outgoing NBA transitions of $q_{B,j}$ have already been generated and represented by the existing successor structure in the plan graph. Hence, these transitions and their corresponding successor plan nodes do not need to be generated again. Instead, \textsc{Reuse\_Successors} examines the successors previously generated from plan nodes associated with the same NBA state $q_{B,j}$ and the same planning status as $\nu_j$. Only successor nodes previously generated from plan nodes with the same
planning status as $\nu_j$ are considered.

The process of \textsc{Reuse\_Successors} is shown in Alg.ref{\label{alg:reuse_successors}}.
Consider an existing successor plan node $\nu_h$ satisfying
$\nu_h.q_B=q_{B,h}$ and $\nu_h.\mathrm{Prog}=\nu_j.\mathrm{Prog}$, where $(q_{B,j},\sigma_{jh},q_{B,h})\in\rightarrow_B$ is an outgoing transition of $q_{B,j}$.
Since $\nu_j$ may store different predicted robot positions and completion times from the plan node
previously used to construct $\nu_h$, the subtask $\xi_{jh}$ is reevaluated from $\nu_j$ using the same greedy allocation procedure as in \textsc{Extend\_PlanGraph}. If no feasible assignment exists, $\nu_h$ cannot be reached from $\nu_j$ and no edge is added. Otherwise, $(\nu_j,\nu_h)$ is added to $\rightarrow_P$, and the resulting child cost is compared with the currently stored cost $J(\nu_h)$. If a lower cost is obtained through $\nu_j$, $\operatorname{par}(\nu_h)$ is updated to $\nu_j$, and
$\nu_h.\mathcal C$, $\nu_h.X$, and $\nu_h.\Theta$ are updated according to the new greedy allocation. Since the updated execution state of $\nu_h$ may affect the allocation and cost of subsequent subtasks, the corresponding updates are further propagated to the
successor plan nodes reachable from $\nu_h$ using the same procedure. Otherwise, the representative predecessor and stored execution states of $\nu_h$ and its subsequent plan nodes remain unchanged.




\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{Reuse\_Successors}}
\label{alg:reuse_successors}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Plan graph $G_P$, plan node $\nu_j$ associated with $q_{B,j}\in Q_B^{\rm exp}$, robot team $\mathcal R$, and workspace $\mathcal W$.
\ENSURE Updated plan graph \(G_P\).
\FORALL{successor NBA state $q_{B,h}$ of $q_{B,j}$}
     \STATE Obtain the plan-node set $\mathcal V_h \gets \{\nu_h\in V_P \mid \nu_h.q_B=q_{B,h},\ \nu_h.\mathrm{Prog}= \nu_j.\mathrm{Prog}\}.$
    \FORALL{$\nu_h\in\mathcal V_h$}
        \STATE Evaluate $\xi_{jh}$ from $\nu_j$ using the greedy allocation procedure in \textsc{Extend\_PlanGraph}.
        \IF{no feasible assignment exists}
            \STATE \text{continue}
        \ENDIF
        \STATE Add $(\nu_j,\nu_h)$ to $\rightarrow_P$
        \STATE Compute the resulting candidate cost
$\widehat J(\nu_h)$ according to Eq.~\ref{eq:plan-node-cost}
\IF{$\widehat J(\nu_h)<J(\nu_h)$}
    \STATE Set $\operatorname{par}(\nu_h)\gets\nu_j$.
    \STATE Update $\nu_h.\mathcal C$, $\nu_h.X$, and
    $\nu_h.\Theta$ according to the new greedy allocation.
    \STATE \textsc{Reuse\_Successors}
    $(G_P,\nu_h,\mathcal R,\mathcal W)$.
\ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Residual-Obligation Scoring of NBA States}
\label{subsec:residual_score}
After a feasible nonaccepting plan node $\nu_j$ associated with a newly generated NBA state $q_{B,j}$ is obtained, we assign it a score that accounts for both the current partial-plan cost and the estimated effort required to satisfy the remaining task requirements. The resulting score guides the expansion of the plan graph by prioritizing the NBA states associated with more promising successor plan nodes, so that the corresponding plan-graph branches are expanded earlier.

Estimating the remaining task effort is nontrivial during on-the-fly construction because the future subtasks are not yet fully known before the complete NBA is generated.To estimate the remaining task effort without constructing the complete NBA, we first extract the positive action propositions that must  be satisfied eventually from the abstract syntax tree $\mathcal D_\varphi$.  For each formula $\psi$, let $\mathsf{ME}(\psi)\subseteq\mathcal A$ denote the corresponding action set. It is computed recursively as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\psi=\top$ or $\psi=\bot$, then
$\mathsf{ME}(\psi)=\emptyset$.
\item If $\psi=a$ with $a\in\mathcal A$, then
$\mathsf{ME}(\psi)=\{a\}$.
If $\psi=p$ with $p\in\mathcal P$, or
$\psi=\neg\pi$ with $\pi\in\mathcal{AP}$, then
$\mathsf{ME}(\psi)=\emptyset$.
Thus, only positive action propositions are retained.
\item If \(\psi=\psi_1\wedge\psi_2\), then $  \mathsf{ME}(\psi)=\mathsf{ME}(\psi_1)\cup \mathsf{ME}(\psi_2).$
    Since both subformulas must be satisfied, the action propositions are collected from both sides.
\item If \(\psi=\psi_1\vee\psi_2\), then $  \mathsf{ME}(\psi)= \mathsf{ME}(\psi_1)\cap \mathsf{ME}(\psi_2). $
    Since either branch may be selected, only action propositions that are unavoidable in both branches are kept.
\item If \(\psi=\bigcirc\psi_1\), then $\mathsf{ME}(\psi)=\mathsf{ME}(\psi_1). $ The next operator shifts the satisfaction time but does not change the unavoidable positive action propositions.
\item If
  \(\psi=\psi_1\,\mathcal U\,\psi_2\) or
  \(\psi=\psi_1\,\mathcal V\,\psi_2\), then
  \(\mathsf{ME}(\psi)=\mathsf{ME}(\psi_2)\), where
  \(\mathcal V\) denotes the release operator. In both cases, the
  positive action propositions retained from the right operand must be satisfied.
\end{enumerate}

The action set $\mathsf{ME}(\varphi)$ is computed once before the plan-graph construction. Let $\mathcal M_i$ denote the remaining action set associated with a plan node $\nu_i$. At the root node, it is initialized as $\mathcal M_0=\mathsf{ME}(\varphi)$. When a feasible successor plan node $\nu_j$ is generated, its remaining set is obtained by aggregating those of its parent nodes and removing the positive actions executed by the current subtask. For every robot $r_k\in\mathcal R_{\tau(a)}$, its predicted arrival time at $l(a)$ is computed from the execution state stored in $\nu_j$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:predicted_arrival}. Let $t_j(a)$ denote the $n(a)$-th smallest finite arrival time, representing the earliest predicted time at which $n(a)$ robots of the required type can reach $l(a)$. If fewer than $n(a)$ robots have finite arrival times, we set $t_j(a)=+\infty$. We then define the residual-obligation estimate $H(\nu_j)$ as
\begin{equation}
H(\nu_j)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\max_{a\in\mathcal M_j} t_j(a),
& \mathcal M_j\neq\emptyset,\\[2mm]
J(\nu_j),
& \mathcal M_j=\emptyset.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:residual_pressure}
\end{equation}
Combining this estimate with the current partial-plan cost gives the score
\begin{equation}
S(\nu_j) = \max\{J(\nu_j),H(\nu_j)\}.
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
A smaller $S(\nu_j)$ indicates a plan node with both a lower current cost and a smaller estimated burden for the remaining actions. Accordingly, if multiple feasible plan nodes generated in the current expansion are associated with the same NBA state $q_{B,j}$, the smallest score among them is retained as $s_B(q_{B,j})$. The states
in $Q_B^{\rm new}$ are then ordered by $s_B$ before being inserted into $Q_B^{\rm open}$, thereby giving earlier expansion to the corresponding promising branches of the plan graph.

\subsection{Plan-Graph Simplification}
\label{subsec:final_pruning}

After the plan graph has been constructed, nodes and edges that cannot contribute to an accepting plan are removed through reachability-based simplification.
The simplification retains only the plan nodes that are both reachable from the root node \(\nu_0\) and capable of reaching a plan node with \(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}\). These nodes are identified by combining forward reachability from \(\nu_0\) along the outgoing edges in \(\rightarrow_P\) with backward reachability from all nodes with \(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}\) along the incoming edges. All other plan nodes are removed together with their incident edges. An edge is retained only if both of its endpoints are retained.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:plan-graph-simplification}
Plan-graph simplification preserves every feasible accepting plan represented in \(G_P\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every feasible accepting plan in \(G_P\) corresponds to a path from the root node \(\nu_0\) to a plan node with
\(\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}\). Every node on this path is reachable from \(\nu_0\) and can reach the accepting node. Hence, every node and edge on the path is retained by the reachability test, and the feasible accepting plan is preserved after simplification.
\end{proof}
\subsection{Plan Extraction}
\label{subsubsec:plan_extraction}
When a plan node $\nu$ with $\nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$ is reached, the corresponding plan is recovered by following the representative-predecessor chain $\operatorname{par}(\nu)$ from $\nu_f$ back to the root node $\nu_0$ and reversing the resulting sequence. Although a plan node may have multiple parents due to node
reuse, $\operatorname{par}(\nu)$ identifies the predecessor from which its stored assignment and predicted execution state were obtained. Therefore, the extracted sequence is consistent with the stored $\mathcal C$, $X$, and $\Theta$ and defines the corresponding executable plan.

\section{BRANCH-AND-BOUND OPTIMIZATION}
\label{sec:bnb_optimization}
After the plan graph has been constructed,
$\Pi_{\rm plan}$ provides an initial feasible solution for the subsequent branch-and-bound (BnB) optimization. The BnB search is performed based on $G_P$,  jointly exploring root-to-accepting paths and feasible robot assignments for the subtasks along each path, with the objective of minimizing $J_\varphi$ in Eq.~\eqref{objective}. The plan $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ initializes the BnB incumbent $(\Pi^\star,J^\star)$, where $\Pi^\star$ denotes the best feasible
plan found so far and $J^\star$ its corresponding cost. The BnB search then improves this incumbent by exploring alternative paths and feasible robot assignments in $G_P^{-}$. If the search is terminated early by a runtime or memory limit, the best solution found so far is returned.


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{BnB\_Optimization}}
\label{alg:bnb}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Plan graph $G_P=(V_P,\rightarrow_P)$ and plan-graph solution $\Pi_{\rm plan}$.
\ENSURE Best plan found $\Pi^\star$ and its cost $J^\star$.
\STATE $\Pi^\star\gets\Pi_{\rm plan}$ and
$J^\star\gets T(\Pi_{\rm plan})$.
\STATE Compute $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu)$ for all
$\nu\in V_P$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:must_actions}.
\STATE Initialize the root BnB node $\mu_0$.
\STATE Insert $\mu_0$ into a priority queue $\mathcal{Q}$ with key $LB(\mu_0)$.
\WHILE{$\mathcal{Q}\neq\emptyset$ and the stopping criterion is not met}
    \STATE $\mu_i\gets\textsc{pop}(\mathcal Q)$.    
    \IF{$LB(\mu_i)\ge J^\star$}
        \STATE \text{break}
    \ENDIF
    \IF{$\mu_i.\nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$}
        \IF{$J(\mu_i)<J^\star$}
            \STATE Update $(\Pi^\star,J^\star)$.
        \ENDIF
        \STATE \text{continue}
    \ENDIF

    \FORALL{$\nu'$ such that $(\mu_i.\nu,\nu')\in\rightarrow_P$}
        \FORALL{feasible robot assignments for $\nu'.\xi$}
            \STATE Generate a child node $\mu_j$ with
            $\mu_j.\nu\gets\nu'$ and $\mu_j.\mathcal C^b$ given by the current assignment.
            \STATE  Update $\mu_j.X^b$ and $\mu_j.\Theta^b$.
            \IF{$LB(\mu_j)\geq J^\star$}
                \STATE \text{continue}
            \ENDIF
            \IF{$LB(\mu_j)<J^\star$}
                \STATE Insert $\mu_j$ into $\mathcal Q$ with key
                $LB(\mu_j)$.
            \ENDIF
            \STATE Use a greedy rollout from $\mu_j$ to update  $(\Pi^\star,J^\star)$.
            % \IF{$LB(\mu_j)<J^\star$}
            %     \STATE Insert $\mu_j$ into $\mathcal{Q}$ with key $LB(\mu_j)$.
            %     \STATE Use a greedy rollout from $\mu_j$ to update
            %     $(\Pi^\star,J^\star)$ if a better feasible plan is found.
            % \ENDIF
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN $(\Pi^\star,J^\star)$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For the root BnB node $\mu_0$, $\mu_0.\nu  = \nu_0$, $\mu_0.X^b$ contains the initial robot positions,
$\mu_0.\Theta^b=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_r}$, and $\mu_0.\mathcal C^b=\emptyset$.

The expansion of  BnB node $\mu_i$ follows the outgoing edges of $\mu_i.\nu$ in the plan graph. For each edge
$(\mu_i.\nu,\nu')\in \rightarrow_P$, the BnB search enumerates every feasible robot assignment for the subtask $\nu'.\xi$. For each feasible assignment, a child BnB node $\mu_j$ is generated with $\mu_j.\nu=\nu'$  and $\mu_j.\mathcal C^b$ given by the corresponding assignment. The robot positions and completion times in $\mu_j.X^b$ and
$\mu_j.\Theta^b$ are then updated similiar to
Sec.~\ref{gen}. Specifically, for each assigned robot, its position is updated to the corresponding location of the action proposition, and its completion time is
updated according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:subtask_completion_time}, while unassigned robots retain their positions and completion times from $\mu_i$.

\subsection{Lower Bound}

For a plan node $\nu\in V_P$, let
$\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu)\subseteq\mathcal A$ denote the set of positive action propositions that must occur in every accepting continuation from $\nu$. For a terminal plan node with $\nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$,
$\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu)=\emptyset$. For every other plan node, the set satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu) =
\bigcap_{(\nu,\nu')\in \rightarrow_P}
\left( \mathcal A(\nu'.\xi) \cup \mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu') \right).
\label{eq:must_actions}
\end{equation}
An action is included in $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu)$ only if it is required by every accepting continuation from $\nu$. These sets can be computed once from the simplified plan graph before the BnB search. For a BnB node $\mu_i$, its current makespan is defined as follows.
\begin{equation}
J(\mu_i)
=
\max_{r_k\in R}\mu_i.\Theta^b(r_k).
\label{eq:bnb_current_cost}
\end{equation}
For each $a\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\mu_i.\nu)$ with
$c(a)=(\tau(a),n(a),l(a))$, the predicted arrival times of the type-compatible robots are calculated from
$\mu_i.X^b$ and $\mu_i.\Theta^b$ according to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:predicted_arrival}. Let $h_a(\mu_i)$ denote the $n(a)$-th smallest finite arrival time among these robots. If fewer than $n(a)$ type-compatible robots can reach $l(a)$, then $h_a(\mu_i)=+\infty$. By relaxing temporal-order constraints and the constraint that a robot cannot execute multiple actions simultaneously, the lower bound of $\mu_i$ is then defined as
\begin{equation}
LB(\mu_i)
=
\max
\left\{
J(\mu_i),
\max_{a\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\mu_i.\nu)}
h_a(\mu_i)
\right\}.
\label{eq:bnb_lower_bound}
\end{equation}
If $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\mu_i.\nu)=\emptyset$, $LB(\mu_i)=J(\mu_i)$.



\subsection{Upper Bound}

After feasible child BnB node is generated, a greedy rollout is performed from that node to construct a feasible completion. The resulting plan cost provides an upper bound and is used to update the incumbent if an improvement is found. Motivated by resource-aware priority rules used to construct feasible upper bounds \cite{Brucker1998RCPSP,Liu2023BnB}, the rollout ranks successor plan nodes according to the robot demand of their remaining subtasks. This favors continuations with lower total robot demand while giving priority to more demanding immediate subtasks,thereby heuristically addressing potential resource bottlenecks earlier.

Consider a node $\mu_i$ and a successor plan node
$\nu'$ satisfying $(\mu_i.\nu,\nu')\in\rightarrow_P$. The normalized robot demand of the immediate subtask is defined as
\begin{equation}
D(\nu'.\xi) =
\sum_{a\in\mathcal A(\nu'.\xi)}
\frac{n(a)}{|\mathcal R_{\tau(a)}|}.
\label{eq:rollout_immediate_demand}
\end{equation}

Here, $n(a)/|\mathcal R_{\tau(a)}|$ represents the fraction of available robots of the required type demanded by action $a$. The normalized demand associated with the corresponding remaining subtasks $W(\nu')$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
W(\nu')
=
\sum_{a\in
\mathcal A(\nu'.\xi)\cup\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu')}
\frac{n(a)}{|\mathcal R_{\tau(a)}|}.
\label{eq:rollout_total_demand}
\end{equation}

The successor priority $K(\nu')$ is then defined as follows.
\begin{equation}
K(\nu')
=
\bigl(W(\nu'),-D(\nu'.\xi)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The first component favors continuations with lower overall robot demand, while the second gives priority to a more demanding immediate subtask when the first components are equal. Remaining ties are resolved by generation order. This rule heuristically favors less resource-intensive continuations while addressing more demanding immediate subtasks earlier to reduce potential resource bottlenecks.

The outgoing successors are considered in increasing lexicographic order of $K(\nu')$. A greedy robot assignment is computed for each candidate until a feasible one is obtained, and the corresponding child becomes the current rollout node. If none of the successors
admits a feasible greedy assignment, the rollout terminates. When a node satisfying $\mu_i.\nu.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$ is reached, the resulting feasible plan updates $(\Pi^\star,J^\star)$ if its cost is smaller than $J^\star$.

\subsection{Branching and Pruning}
Let the priority queue $\mathcal Q$ store the generated but unexpanded BnB nodes in nondecreasing order of their lower bounds. At each iteration, the node with the smallest $LB$ is selected for expansion. A child node is discarded if its lower bound satisfies $LB(\mu_i)\geq J^\star$, since no accepting completion from that node
can improve the incumbent. If no feasible robot assignment exists for a successor subtask, the corresponding child node is not generated.
The search terminates when $\mathcal Q$ is empty or when
$\min_{\mu\in\mathcal Q}LB(\mu)\geq J^\star$. In either case, no unexplored node can yield a better feasible plan, and the incumbent is therefore optimal over the paths and robot assignments represented in $G_P^{-}$. If a prescribed runtime or memory limit is reached earlier, the
best incumbent found so far is returned.


\section{Plan Adaptation under Robot Unavailability}
\label{sec:plan_adaptation}
During execution, a robot may become unavailable because of an unexpected failure and can no longer participate in the remaining task. The unfinished subtasks must therefore be reassigned to the available robots.

When robot unavailability is detected, the unavailable robots are removed from the team, and the current task progress and robot states define a new root for replanning. Since the previous plan may no longer
be feasible, the BnB search is initialized without a warm start and re-optimizes the unfinished subtasks over the retained plan graph using the updated robot team. A greedy rollout is applied during the search to obtain a feasible repaired plan quickly and provide an incumbent for
subsequent BnB optimization. The resulting repaired plan replaces the unexecuted portion of the original plan.

\section{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

This section analyzes the computational complexity of the proposed framework in three stages: GBA-level pruning, on-the-fly plan-graph construction, and BnB optimization.

The feasibility pruning during GBA construction is linear in the total number of action requirements contained in the candidate transitions.Let $\max_{q_g\in Q_g}\deg^+(q_g)$ denote the maximum out-degree of the GBA. After the acceptance-progress information is computed for all GBA transitions, the progress-consistent decomposable-transition pruning has complexity $O(|\rightarrow_g||\mathcal F_g|+|\mathcal Q_g|(\max_{q_g\in \mathcal  Q_g}\deg^+(q_g))^3(|\mathcal{AP}|+|\mathcal F_g|))$.

The main cost of plan-graph construction comes from greedy assignment and plan-node processing. For a parent node $\nu_i$ and $\nu_i.\xi$, greedy assignment requires
$O(\sum_{a_\ell\in\mathcal A(\nu_i.\xi)}
|\mathcal R_{\tau(a_\ell)}|\log|\mathcal R_{\tau(a_\ell)}|+n_r)$. The residual-obligation score requires
comparable arrival-time calculations for the actions in $\mathcal M_j$. Searching for a compatible existing plan node costs $O(|\rightarrow_P|)$ in the worst case. For each expanded NBA state, sorting the pending successor
set $P_B$ costs $O(|P_B|\log|P_B|)$. If an improved representative execution state is propagated to its descendants, the propagation can traverse at most the retained plan graph and therefore costs $O(|V_P|+|\rightarrow_P|)$ in the worst case. Plan-graph simplification also requires $O(|V_P|+|\rightarrow_P|)$.

The BnB stage enumerates feasible robot assignments along the retained plan-graph edges. For an edge $(\nu_i,\nu_j)\in \rightarrow_P$, the number of candidate assignments is upper bounded by $\prod_{a_\ell\in\mathcal A(\nu_j.\xi)}
\binom{|\mathcal R_{\tau(a_\ell)}|}{n(a_\ell)}$, leading to exponential growth in the worst case. However, pruning can substantially reduce the number of BnB nodes explored in practice.Evaluating $LB(\mu_i)$ requires $O(\sum_{a_\ell\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\mu_i.\nu)}|\mathcal R_{\tau_\ell}|\log|\mathcal R_{\tau_\ell}|+n_r)$. The sets $\mathcal A_{\mathrm{must}}(\nu)$ are precomputed once and reused during the search. The greedy rollout avoids exhaustive assignment enumeration, and each priority-queue operation costs $O(\log|\mathcal Q|)$.

Overall, although the worst-case complexity remains exponential, GBA-level pruning reduces the automaton structure propagated to subsequent stages, while the on-the-fly construction enables a feasible plan to be obtained before the complete NBA is generated. These
features reduce the practical computational burden.

\section{THEORETICAL ANALYSIS}
\label{sec:theoretical_analysis}


\begin{lemma}\label{lem:initial-completeness}
Under Assumption \ref{assum:motion}, the on-the-fly plan-graph construction is sound and complete: it returns a feasible plan whenever one exists, and every returned plan satisfies $\varphi$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Suppose that a feasible plan exists. By Lemma~\ref{l3}, a feasible accepting run is preserved after GBA pruning. We then show inductively that the corresponding feasible extensions are
preserved in the plan graph. The root node $\nu_0$ gives the base
case. Suppose that a feasible plan node $\nu_i$ on the accepting run
has been reached, and consider its next successor $\nu_j$. Since the
induced subtask $\nu_j.\xi$ is feasible, for each robot type $\tau$,
$\sum_{a_\ell\in\mathcal A(\nu_j.\xi),\,\tau(a_\ell)=\tau}
n(a_\ell)\leq |\mathcal R_\tau|$. Under Assumption \ref{assum:motion}, after excluding the regions prohibited by the holding condition and negative propositions, every required region $l(a_\ell)$ remains reachable; hence $d_k(x_i(r_k),l(a_\ell))<\infty$ for $r_k\in\mathcal R_{\tau(a_\ell)}$. When the actions in $\mathcal A(\nu_j.\xi)$ are processed sequentially, the number of unassigned robots of type $\tau(a_\ell)$ before assigning $a_\ell$ is $|\mathcal R_{\tau(a_\ell)}|-\sum_{a_{\ell'},\,\tau(a_{\ell'})=\tau(a_\ell)}n(a_{\ell'})$, which is no smaller than $n(a_\ell)$. Therefore, the greedy assignment succeeds and the edge $(\nu_i,\nu_j)\in\rightarrow_P$ is generated. Thus, the plan graph contains the complete finite prefix--suffix path and reaches a node $\nu_f$ with $\nu_f.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:plan-graph-simplification}, this accepting path is preserved by plan-graph simplification.

Conversely, every extracted plan follows the representative-predecessor chain from $\nu_0$ to a node $\nu_f$ satisfying $\nu_f.\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$. Each edge on this chain corresponds to an NBA transition, and its stored assignment satisfies the requirements of the associated subtask. Hence, the resulting NBA run is an accepting prefix--suffix run, and its induced word satisfies $\varphi$. Therefore, every extracted plan is feasible and satisfies $\varphi$.

\end{proof}



\begin{theorem}\label{thm:soundness_completeness}
Under the Assumption \ref{assum:motion}, the proposed framework is sound and complete: it returns a feasible plan whenever one exists, and every returned plan satisfies $\varphi$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{l3}, GBA pruning preserves the existence of a feasible solution. Under Assumption~\ref{assum:motion},
Lemma~\ref{lem:initial-completeness} guarantees that, whenever a feasible plan exists, the constructed plan graph contains a feasible accepting plan and $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ satisfies $\varphi$.  Therefore, it remains only to show that the subsequent BnB optimization preserves these properties.
The BnB stage initializes its incumbent with $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ and replaces it only by a feasible plan reaching a node with $\mathrm{Prog}=\mathrm{acc}$. Since every BnB extension follows an edge in $\rightarrow_P$ and uses a feasible robot assignment, every updated incumbent also corresponds to an accepting prefix--suffix run and satisfies $\varphi$. If no improved plan is found, $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ is retained. Therefore, the proposed framework returns a feasible plan whenever one exists, and every returned plan satisfies $\varphi$.

The same argument applies to online replanning. After unavailable robots are removed, the retained plan graph is re-rooted at the current task progress and the BnB search re-enumerates feasible assignments over the remaining robot team. Robot unavailability can only eliminate
previously feasible assignments and does not introduce new plan-graph transitions. Hence, if a feasible completion of the remaining task exists in the retained plan graph, the replanning procedure finds one when allowed to terminate normally, and every returned repaired plan corresponds to an accepting continuation and satisfies the remaining
task requirements.
\end{proof}

\subsection{First-Solution Quality}\label{sec:first_solution}
To address question (ii), we examine the quality of the first feasible solution obtained during on-the-fly plan-graph construction. In particular, we evaluate whether the residual-obligation score can guide the search toward a high-quality solution at an early stage. For comparison, we use the original LTL2BA\cite{Gastin2001FastLT} discovery order instead of the residual-obligation score, while keeping the remaining planning procedure unchanged. We consider four LTL specifications.
\begin{align*}
\begin{autobreak}
\varphi_1 =
\Diamond(a_2 \wedge \Diamond a_1)
\wedge \Diamond\bigl(a_3 \wedge \Diamond(a_5 \wedge \neg p_4)
\wedge \Diamond a_4
\wedge \Diamond a_6
\wedge \Diamond a_7

\wedge \Diamond a_3
\wedge \Diamond(a_3 \vee a_4)
\wedge \Diamond a_7
\wedge \Box\Diamond a_5,
\end{autobreak}\\[1mm]
\begin{autobreak}

\Diamond a_9
\wedge \Diamond a_{10}

\wedge \Diamond a_5

\subsection{Branch-and-Bound Optimization} \label{bnb_exp}
We further evaluate the BnB optimization on the  plan graph, focusing on solution improvement and pruning effectiveness.
Consider a warehouse inspection task specified by $\varphi=\Diamond a_1\wedge\Diamond a_2\wedge \Diamond(a_3\wedge\Diamond(a_6\wedge\Diamond(a_4\vee a_5))) \wedge\Diamond a_7\wedge \Box\Diamond a_9\wedge \Box\Diamond a_{10}$. The robot-team configuration and action requirements are the same as in Sec.~\ref{sec:first_solution}.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{bnb}, the plan-graph solution $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ provides an initial incumbent of $J^\star=47.21\,\mathrm{s}$ before the BnB search begins. This warm start makes incumbent-based pruning available from the beginning of the search: the first node satisfying
$LB\geq J^\star$ is pruned at $0.29\,\mathrm{s}$. The remaining nodes are explored in nondecreasing order of their lower bounds, while the greedy completion of each node provides an upper bound. Whenever a feasible completion with a lower cost is found, the incumbent
$J^\star$ is updated.
The best incumbent is first obtained at $2.37\,\mathrm{s}$, reducing the completion cost from $47.21\,\mathrm{s}$ to $43.32 \mathrm{s}$. Over the complete BnB search, 122362 of the 138145 generated assignment nodes are pruned, corresponding to a pruning ratio of $88.58\%$. These results show that $\Pi_{\rm plan}$ serves not only as an early feasible solution but also as an effective warm start for subsequent optimization: its incumbent enables early lower-bound pruning.

\subsection{Scalability}
To address question (iii), we evaluate the scalability of the proposed method with respect to robot-team size and task complexity.  We compare the proposed method with two representative LTL-MRTA approaches: the poset-based
method~\cite{liu2024time} and the MILP-based method~\cite{luo2022temporal}. Three warehouse inspection
specifications of increasing complexity are considered:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_1 ={}&
\Diamond a_1
\wedge \Diamond a_2
\wedge \Diamond(a_3 \wedge \Diamond a_4)
\wedge \Diamond(a_5 \wedge \neg p_7)\\
&\wedge \Diamond a_6
\wedge \Diamond a_9
\wedge \Box(\neg a_9 \,\mathcal{U}\, a_6),\\
\varphi_2 ={}&
\Diamond a_1
\wedge \Diamond a_2
\wedge \Diamond(a_3 \wedge \Diamond(a_6 \wedge \Diamond(a_4 \vee a_5)))\\
&\wedge \Diamond a_7
\wedge \Diamond(a_9 \wedge \Diamond a_{10}),\\
\varphi_3 ={}&
\Diamond(a_1 \wedge \Diamond a_2)
\wedge \Diamond a_3
\wedge \Diamond a_6
\wedge \Diamond(a_4 \vee a_5)\\
&\wedge \Diamond a_8
\wedge \Diamond a_7
\wedge \Diamond a_9
\wedge \Diamond a_{10}
\wedge (\neg a_9 \mathcal{U} a_2).
\end{aligned}
\]
The corresponding action requirements are: $ c(a_1)=(1,2,l_1), c(a_2)=(2,n,l_2), c(a_3)=(2,1,l_3), c(a_4)=(1,\lfloor n/2 \rfloor,l_4), c(a_5)=(5,\lceil 2n/3 \rceil,l_5), c(a_6)=(3,\max\{2,\lceil 0.75n\rceil\},l_6), c(a_7)=(1,n,l_7),c(a_8)=(1,2,l_8), c(a_9)=(3,1,l_8), c(a_{10})=(4,1,l_8).$ A $5\times n$ configuration contains five robot types with $n$ robots of each type. The corresponding NBA sizes are reported in Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison} to indicate the complexity of the specifications. The required team sizes of several collaborative actions are scaled with $n$ to maintain increasing collaboration demands as the robot team grows. For each specification and team configuration, 25 runs are performed with randomly generated initial robot positions. The same
initialization is used for all three methods within each run, and the reported results are averaged over the 25 runs.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:scalability_tfirst} and Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison}, the proposed method consistently obtains feasible plans substantially earlier than the two baseline methods. In particular, $T_{\mathrm{first}}$ remains at the single-digit-second level across all tested cases, whereas the baseline methods require tens or even hundreds of seconds for the more complex specifications. Increasing the robot-team size has only a moderate effect on $T_{\mathrm{first}}$ for both the proposed and poset-based
methods. However, as the specification becomes more complex from $\varphi_1$ to $\varphi_3$, the $T_{\mathrm{first}}$ of the poset-based method increases considerably. This is because task-order information
must first be extracted from the NBA and at least one poset must be constructed before planning can produce a feasible solution. The MILP-based method is more sensitive to increases in both task and team sizes, as task-allocation decisions and temporal constraints are jointly encoded in the mixed-integer formulation, resulting in an increasingly large combinatorial search space.

By contrast, the proposed method performs task allocation during NBA generation and can return a feasible plan as soon as an accepting path is reached. This avoids waiting for complete automaton processing or separate task-structure extraction before planning begins, contributing to the relatively mild growth of
$T_{\mathrm{first}}$ as the problem size increases. A similar advantage is observed for $T_{\mathrm{best}}$, showing that the proposed method not only obtains a feasible plan early but also improves it rapidly
across the tested instances. For the largest instances, the subsequent BnB search can still incur a high total search cost because of the combinatorial growth of robot assignments; however, this mainly affects $T_{\mathrm{search}}$ rather than the early availability of feasible
and high-quality plans.

\subsection{Online Task Reallocation Under Robot Unavailability}
We evaluate the online replanning capability of the proposed framework under robot unavailability. The LTL specification and robot-team configuration are the same as those in Sec.~\ref{bnb_exp}.


During execution, robot $r_{20}$ becomes unavailable at
$t=20 \mathrm{s}$, triggering replanning. The unavailable robot is removed from the team, and the retained plan graph is re-rooted according to the current task progress and robot states to search for a feasible continuation. The robot initial positions and failure time are kept identical across 25 runs. On average, the first feasible
repaired plan is obtained in $0.0457 \mathrm{s}$, and the best repaired plan is found in $0.3903 \mathrm{s}$. Figure~\ref{dynamic} illustrates the resulting reassignment. Before $r_{20}$ becomes unavailable, $a_5$ is assigned to robots $r_{20}$, $r_{21}$, $r_{22}$, and $r_{24}$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{dynamic}(a). After the failure, $r_{23}$ is reassigned to $a_5$ and travels to $l_5$ to replace $r_{20}$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{dynamic}(b). The repaired plan therefore restores the
four-robot collaboration required for executing $a_5$.

% During task execution, robot \(r_{20}\) becomes unavailable at \(t=20 \mathrm{s}\), which triggers replanning. The robot initial positions and failure time are kept identical across all runs. We run the
% replanning procedure 25 times. The average times required to obtain the first feasible repaired plan and the best repaired plan found by BnB are
% \(45.74\,\mathrm{ms}\) and \(390.32\,\mathrm{ms}\), respectively. As shown in Fig.~\ref{dynamic}(a), \(a_5\) was initially assigned to robots \(r_{20}\), \(r_{21}\), \(r_{22}\), and \(r_{24}\). After \(r_{20}\) becomes unavailable, \(r_{23}\) replaces it and travels to \(l_5\), as shown in Fig.~\ref{dynamic}(b). The repaired assignment therefore retains the four robots required to execute \(a_5\).
% These results show that reusing the retained plan graph enables rapid task reassignment while preserving the collaboration requirement of the unfinished action.
%  Two robot-removal events are triggered: robot 0 is removed at $t=15.0$ s and robot 20 is removed at $t=30.0$ s.

% The proposed workflow generates repaired first solutions within 45.738 ms and 1.652 ms
% for the two events, respectively. The corresponding best repaired solutions are obtained within 69.032 ms and 33.599 ms. As shown in Fig. \ref{dynamic}, during execution, agent 0 breaks down at \(t=15\,\mathrm{s}\) and is removed from the available team. The planner then updates the current execution state and continues the remaining task allocation with the surviving agents. A more severe failure occurs at \(t=30\,\mathrm{s}\), where Agent 20 also becomes unavailable. After this event, the online adaptation module re-identifies the current planning state and resumes the branch-and-bound search from the corresponding node. The unfinished subtasks are then reassigned to the remaining 23 agents. As shown in the timeline figure, no subsequent task segment is assigned to the failed agents, while the remaining agents continue or take over tasks such as \(p_1,p_5\), and \(p_6\). The replanning process preserves the task ordering and collaboration constraints throughout execution. In particular, tasks that are still feasible with the current collaborators continue execution, whereas tasks affected by failed agents are reassigned and executed by other compatible agents. The online search first obtains a feasible solution with cost \(71\), and further improves it to \(62\). Despite the two agent failures, all required tasks are eventually completed at approximately \(62\,\mathrm{s}\), demonstrating that the proposed adaptation method can maintain feasibility and improve the execution plan under unexpected team-size reductions.

\section{Physical experiments}
\label{sec:physical_experiments}
The physical experiments in this section are conducted to validate the proposed framework in a real-world environment. TurtleBot3 robots\cite{8675050} are used to perform the LTL-MRTA tasks in a laboratory environment. The system runs Python 3.8.10 on Ubuntu 20.04 and the ROS\cite{Quigley2009ROS} version is Noetic. Each physical experiment was repeated five times, and the average performance is reported. The experiments are performed on a laptop with Intel Core i7-11390H CPU and 16 GB RAM.

\subsection{Experiment Setup}
The experimental workspace contains five regions of inter-est $\mathcal{L} = \{l_1, l_2, l_3, l_4, l_5\}$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{static}(a). Regions $l_1$ and $l_4$ serve as the inbound and outbound areas, respectively; regions $l_2$ and $l_5$ are used for goods storage; and region $l_3$ is the central control area.  The robotic team consists of four robots, denoted by $r_1$, $r_2$, $r_3$, and $r_4$, where $r_1$ and $r_2$ are Type-1 robots, while $r_3$ and $r_4$ are Type-2 robots. The warehouse inspection task is specified by $\varphi= \Diamond a_1 \land \Diamond \left( a_2 \land \left( \Diamond (a_3 \land \neg a_2) \land \Diamond (a_4 \land \neg a_2 ) \right) \right) \land \Diamond \left(a_5 \land a_6\right).$ $a_1$ represents a Type-1 robot inspecting the quantity of goods in the inbound area;  $a_2$ represents a Type-2 robot traveling to the central control area to obtain goods information;  $a_3$ represents two Type-1 robots collaboratively inspecting the condition of goods in region $l_2$;  $a_4$ represents a Type-1 robot inspecting the condition of goods in region $l_5$;  and $a_5$ and $a_6$ represent a Type-1 robot and a Type-2 robot, respectively, inspecting the quantity of goods in the outbound area.

\subsection{Task Execution}
For the given inspection task $\varphi$, the task is successfully executed, as shown in Fig.~\ref{static}(b) and (c). The first feasible solution is obtained in 0.2168939~s, while the optimal solution is found in 1.290683~s. The complete search is finished in 4.327640~s. The resulting plan executes $a_2$ first, followed by $a_3$, $a_1$,
and $a_4$, and finally performs $a_5$ and $a_6$ jointly.
During execution, robot $r_3$ first travels to the central control area $l_3$ to execute $a_2$ and obtain the goods information. After that, robots $r_1$ and $r_2$ collaboratively inspect the condition of the stored cups and water bottles in $l_2$ by executing $a_3$. Robot $r_2$ then proceeds to the inbound area $l_1$ to count the
incoming cargo boxes by executing $a_1$, while $r_1$ subsequently inspects the water bottles stored in $l_5$
by executing $a_4$. Finally, $r_1$ and $r_4$ jointly count the cargo boxes in the outbound area $l_4$ by executing $a_5$ and $a_6$, respectively. The physical experiment
confirms that the generated plan can be executed by the heterogeneous robot team in the required temporal order while satisfying the collaboration and robot-type requirements of the warehouse inspection task.

Robot $r_2$ then travels to the inbound area $l_1$ to execute $a_1$ and inspect the quantity of goods. Robot $r_1$ travels to region $l_5$ to execute $a_4$ and inspect the condition of the stored goods. Finally, robots $r_1$ and $r_4$ collaboratively perform $a_5$ and $a_6$ in the outbound area $l_4$ to verify the quantity of outgoing goods. The experimental results show that the proposed framework can efficiently generate a feasible execution plan for the given LTL task and successfully coordinate heterogeneous robots to satisfy the task requirements.

\subsection{Task Execution under Robot Failure}
We further evaluate online replanning under robot failure. During execution, $r_3$ becomes unavailable while traveling to $l_3$ to execute $a_2$, which triggers replanning. The unavailable robot is removed from the team, and the retained plan graph is re-rooted at the current task progress and robot states to search for a feasible continuation. The first feasible repaired plan is obtained in $0.0047 \mathrm{s}$, and is also the best plan returned in this experiment. The complete search finishes in $0.0874 \mathrm{s}$. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic}, $r_3$ is initially assigned to $a_2$ and moves toward $l_3$. After its failure, $r_4$, which has the same robot type, is reassigned to $a_2$ and travels to $l_3$. After completing $a_2$, $r_4$ continues to $l_4$ for the remaining outbound inspection task. The experiment demonstrates that the proposed framework can rapidly recover a feasible continuation after robot unavailability while preserving the temporal and  task requirements.



% To deal with unexpected robot failures, suppose that robot $r_3$ fails while traveling to region $l_3$ to execute $a_2$. The failure of $r_3$ triggers the re-planning process. The first feasible solution is obtained in 0.004662~s, which is also the optimal solution, while the complete search is completed in 0.087392~s. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic}(a), robot $r_3$ initially moves toward the central control area $l_3$ to perform $a_2$. However, $r_3$ suddenly fails while traveling to region $l_3$ and can no longer participate in the remaining tasks. The proposed method then re-plans the task allocation, and robot $r_4$, which is also a Type-2 robot, is assigned to replace $r_3$. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamic}(b), $r_4$ travels to region $l_3$ and executes $a_2$ to retrieve the goods information. After completing $a_2$, robot $r_4$ proceeds to the outbound area $l_4$ to participate in the subsequent task execution. The results demonstrate that the proposed framework can rapidly respond to unexpected robot failures by updating the task allocation and generating a new feasible execution plan.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% This paper presented an on-the-fly LTL-MRTA framework that integrates task allocation into the joint construction of the NBA and plan graph. GBA-level pruning and score-guided construction enable the framework to obtain the first feasible plan early, while the branch-and-bound procedure further improves the plan-graph solution. In addition, an online replanning strategy reassigns unfinished tasks when robots become unavailable. Theoretical analysis establishes soundness and completeness of the proposed framework under the stated assumptions.
% Numerical and physical experiments validate the efficiency, solution quality, scalability, and adaptability of the proposed framework.
This paper presented an on-the-fly LTL-MRTA framework for heterogeneous multi-robot systems. The plan graph is constructed synchronously with NBA generation, with task allocation performed incrementally as new NBA transitions become available, enabling feasible plans to be obtained
without waiting for complete NBA construction. GBA-level pruning prevents infeasible and unnecessary structures from being propagated to the NBA, while the residual-obligation score guides the expansion of the
plan graph toward promising branches. The resulting plan-graph solution provides a warm start for subsequent BnB refinement. When robots become unavailable during execution, the retained plan graph is re-rooted at
the current task progress to support online task reallocation. Under the stated assumptions, the theoretical analysis establishes the soundness
and completeness of the proposed framework.
Numerical experiments demonstrate the early acquisition of high-quality feasible plans and favorable scalability with increasing task and robot team sizes. Physical experiments further validate the executability of the generated plans and the capability of the framework to reallocate  tasks under robot failure.
